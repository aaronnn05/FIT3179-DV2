1Who is looking up?

Anyone can rate their night sky from 1 (bright) to 7 (very dark) on the Globe at Night website.

Most ratings come from the big east-coast cities.

One night, 21 June 2020, brought in 4,425 of the 6,432 reports. A national event asked Australians to rate their sky on the longest night of the year.

Try it: drag across the bars to filter the map.

Counted per million people, the order changes.

The Australian Capital Territory comes first, ahead of the Northern Territory and Queensland.

But look at the survey night.

The Australian Capital Territory's lead comes from that one night. On all other nights, the Northern Territory and Queensland report the most.

Where is the sky darkest?

Not near the big cities. Melbourne squares score about 2 and Sydney about 3. The darkest squares are in Tasmania, inland South Australia and western New South Wales.

Most people rate their sky somewhere in the middle.

City lights hide faint stars, so a truly dark sky is rare.

2Where do people meet?

Astronomy clubs follow the population.

New South Wales has 17 clubs. Western Australia, the biggest state, has 2.

Per million people, the Northern Territory comes first.

It has only one club, but so few people that one is enough to top the list.

South Australia and Western Australia trail far behind everyone else.

3What gets people outside?

Of four big sky events, aurora gets the most searches in every state.

Tasmania, the best place in Australia to see one, searches for little else.

Interest stays low until the Sun acts up.

A huge solar storm in May 2024 sent searches to their highest point in five years.

4Do you need a telescope?

No one publishes telescope sales, so we used Google searches instead.

They hardly move, with two exceptions. Both came from the James Webb Space Telescope: its launch in December 2021 and its first pictures in July 2022.

Searches for gear and photography stayed flat.

The growth is in plain "stargazing": people who just want to look.

Earlier spikes lasted a month. This rise has lasted more than two years.

5So, should you look up?

Yes, and you don't need much to start.

Interest is growing among people who just want to look, not buy gear. If you live outside the east coast, there may be no club nearby, so look for the darkest sky you can reach and start there.

Data sources

  • Globe at Night: sky ratings in Australia, 2017 to 2025. Invalid scores and locations removed.
  • IceInSpace: list of astronomy clubs. Club locations are approximate town centres.
  • Google Trends: searches in Australia and by state, downloaded October 2026.
  • Australian Bureau of Statistics: state populations, March 2026.
  • Natural Earth: state boundaries.

Notes

  • Globe at Night ratings come from volunteers, so they show who took part, not everyone who looks up. The 21 June 2020 spike came from a light-pollution mapping event run by the Australasian Dark Sky Alliance.
  • Google Trends scores are relative (100 = busiest point), not counts. Searches are not sales.
  • The waffle chart rounds the 6,432 reports to 100 squares (file: data/sky_score_waffle.csv).
