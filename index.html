Astronomy as a hobby in Australia

Look up, Australia

Sky reports, astronomy clubs, comets and telescopes: what Australians do when they look at the night sky, and where they do it.

1 Who is checking the sky?

Globe at Night asks people to look up, compare their sky with a chart and report a score from 1 (bright) to 7 (dark).

Between 2017 and 2025, Australians sent in 6,432 usable reports. They come from the places where people live: the east coast, Perth and Adelaide. Most of the centre and the north has none.

One night makes the data lumpy. On 21 June 2020, 4,425 reports arrived, 69% of the whole set. Drag across the bars to see the map without that year.

Counting reports per person changes the order. The Australian Capital Territory sends in 537 reports per million residents, the Northern Territory 372 and Queensland 349. Victoria sends in 137.

Small places would vanish on a normal map, so each state and territory is drawn as an equal tile.

Few reports describe a truly dark sky. Only 184 of 6,432 score the maximum of 7, and the most common score is 4. Dim objects are hard to see under a bright sky.

2 Where do people join in?

IceInSpace lists 45 astronomy clubs. 42 give a place, and most of those sit on the east coast.

New South Wales has 17 clubs and Victoria 12. Western Australia, the biggest state, has two.

Divide by population and the Northern Territory comes first: one club for about 269,000 people. South Australia has one club for 1.9 million.

These are small numbers. One new club in the Northern Territory would nearly double its score.

The same figures as a ranked bar chart, which is easier to read exactly. Nothing separates Queensland, Tasmania and Victoria at 1.7 clubs per million.

3 What makes them look up?

Events pull people outside. We compared Google searches for four of them in each state over five years.

Aurora wins everywhere. In Tasmania, 91% of these searches are about aurora. Comets do best in Queensland and Western Australia, where they take 25%.

The four panels share one colour scale, so a darker shade always means a bigger share.

Aurora interest is flat until the sun does something. In May 2024 one of the strongest solar storms of this century pushed searches to their five-year peak. A strong storm in October 2024 gave a smaller peak.

4 What do they look with?

Nobody publishes how many telescopes Australians own. Searches show interest, not sales.

Searches for "telescope" barely move for ten years. One month stands out: July 2022, when the James Webb Space Telescope released its first images.

People who want gear search for gear terms, and those stayed flat. "Astrophotography" went from 8 to 8. The broad term "stargazing" went from 7 to 20 between 2016 and 2025, nearly three times higher.

The growth is in people who just want to look.

Month by month, "stargazing" rose through 2024 and has stayed around 15 to 25 since. Its two earlier spikes, in April 2017 and May 2018, lasted a single month each.

The latest month, October 2026, is incomplete.

5 What this means

Search interest points to growth among casual watchers, and clubs sit where the people are.

Clubs cluster on the east coast, and the rising searches are for the broad term "stargazing", not for gear. A new stargazer in Perth or Adelaide has far fewer clubs to join than one in Sydney or Melbourne.

Data sources

  • Globe at Night: sky darkness reports, Australia, 2017 to 2025. Cleaned to valid scores of 1 to 7 and Australian locations.
  • IceInSpace: list of astronomy clubs. Club locations are approximate town centres.
  • Google Trends: search interest for Australia and by state, downloaded October 2026. Values are relative, not counts.
  • Australian Bureau of Statistics: state populations, March 2026.
  • Natural Earth: state boundaries (public domain).

Notes

  • Globe at Night reports are volunteered, so they show who took part, not how many people look at the sky.
  • Search interest is a proxy. It does not measure telescope ownership or sales.
